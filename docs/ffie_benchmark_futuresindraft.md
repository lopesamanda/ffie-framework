# FFIE vs. Futures in Draft — Benchmarking Analysis

## What Futures in Draft actually is

Futures in Draft (produced by Storythings, supported by the Joseph Rowntree Foundation) is an editorial media platform, not an interactive tool. Its content is organized into six recurring formats: "A World With" (an audio series of imaginative visioning interviews), "Power of Place" (personal essays on place-based resistance and worldbuilding), "Pages for Changes" (curated reading lists from named practitioners), "Worldbuilding" (long-reads connecting systems-change theory to on-the-ground organizing), "How To" (practical action guides), and "Fieldnotes: Ideas in Progress" (informal, in-progress thinking). Every piece is written or curated by a named, credentialed contributor — activists, campaigners, technologists, organizers — with a short bio. There is no "build your own" mechanic anywhere on the site; it is read-only, editorial, and updated on a weekly cadence.

## What's genuinely good here, worth learning from

The Glossary is the single most transferable idea. The site states outright that "terms used to describe this work are often evolving, interconnected and interchangeable" and gives readers a dedicated, always-available place to look words up rather than footnoting every piece. FFIE has exactly this problem with CLA, "diegetic artifact," "Use-Distrust Paradox," and the Critical Feminist Matrix's own vocabulary — a Glossary page (linked from About, and maybe from a small "?" affordance wherever a term first appears in Create) would solve the jargon-drift risk flagged in the earlier product review without diluting any of the language itself.

Named bylines and short bios on every piece give the site warmth and credibility that pure theory can't — a reader trusts "Venetia La Manna, founder of #Offline48" faster than an anonymous paragraph. FFIE's Future Commons is anonymous by design right now, which may be the right call for privacy reasons, but it's worth deciding that deliberately rather than by default — an optional, lightweight attribution (a first name, a role, nothing more) could add some of that same warmth to standout community futures without compromising anyone who prefers to stay anonymous.

The site's tag taxonomy (Abolition, Land justice, Decolonialism, Food sovereignty, Collective care, Border abolition, Cosmologies) is rich and intersectional, and every tag is a live filter into more content. FFIE's Explore filtering is currently scoped to sector and quadrant only — there's room to develop a similarly rich thematic tag layer (pulled from the narrative cards' own tensions, not imported vocabulary) so Explore becomes a place to wander by theme, not just by sector.

Accessibility Statement and Transparency Statement live as permanent footer links. FFIE doesn't currently have either, and given the site's positioning as validated academic research being made public, both would reinforce the credibility the product is already trying to earn through methodology.

A weekly return cadence gives Futures in Draft a reason for a visitor to come back. FFIE's Explore/Future Commons doesn't currently have an equivalent hook — something like a periodically-rotated "a future worth reading" spotlight pulled from real Future Commons submissions could borrow this same repeat-visit logic without needing a content team producing new essays every week.

## The real differential — and why FFIE shouldn't chase parity here

Futures in Draft is a magazine you read; FFIE is an instrument you use. That's not a small stylistic difference, it's the whole product thesis. Futures in Draft has no mechanic that implicates the reader in a future the way FFIE's Create journey does — there is no moment where a Futures in Draft reader has to admit what a technology cost someone, name an artifact's shadow side, or watch their own choices land on an analytical matrix. Futures in Draft persuades through the credibility of real voices; FFIE persuades through the discomfort of participation. Neither is better in the abstract, but they are not competing for the same job — a reader finishes a Futures in Draft essay having learned something about someone else's fight; a user finishes an FFIE session having built and been implicated in a future of their own.

The second differential is empirical grounding at the level of a research instrument: FFIE's replicable unit (persona + diegetic artifact + matrix position) is validated across two workshop cohorts, 134 survey responses, and 23 interviews, and every future generated is structurally comparable to every other one — which is what makes the aggregate views (sector benchmarking, Future Commons patterns) possible at all. Futures in Draft's essays are bespoke and not designed to aggregate into anything larger; there's no equivalent claim being made there, nor does the format support one.

The practical takeaway: adopt the Glossary, the optional-attribution idea, the richer tag taxonomy, and the accessibility/transparency footer links — all genuinely good, low-risk, high-credibility moves. Don't try to become a content magazine competing on volume of essays or contributor voices; FFIE's advantage is that it's the only one of the two that turns a visitor into a co-author of a future, backed by a validated method, and that's worth protecting rather than diluting with an editorial content strategy it isn't built for.
