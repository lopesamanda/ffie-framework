Product Design · Two-sided Marketplace
da.tes
Startup-investor matchmaking platform redesign. The algorithm had no useful signal — both sides were under-delivering data.
RoleLead Designer · Sprint Facilitator
ContextB2B Platform · Brazil · 2023
Duration6-day Sprint + 2 validation rounds
UsersStartups + Investors (two-sided)
ContextDomain understanding + business model awareness

da.tes is a startup-investor matchmaking platform in Brazil. The platform had a working product but a fundamental data problem: match quality was only as good as the profiles on both sides — and both sides were under-delivering.

Startups submitted incomplete profiles. Investors didn't specify their thesis. The result: matches felt arbitrary, users disengaged, and the platform's core value proposition — intelligent connection — wasn't materializing.

Understanding the business model before touching the interface was the first decision. In a two-sided marketplace, data quality is a chicken-and-egg problem: neither side invests until they see a return. The design had to create that return before it existed.

ProblemPrecision of framing, not symptoms

The interface wasn't the problem. Misaligned incentives were.

Startups had no feedback that completeness improved their match score. Investors had no structured way to specify what they actually wanted. Onboarding collected data as if it were a compliance exercise — not a trust-building conversation.

The question wasn't "how do we redesign the form?" It was: how do we make data quality feel immediately valuable to users who have no prior reason to trust the platform?

[ Problem diagram: data quality → match quality → engagement → data quality loop ]
My RoleWhat I owned vs. what I influenced

Lead Designer across Discovery, Strategy, Prototyping, and Validation. Facilitated the 6-day Design Sprint. Led stakeholder interviews for both user types. Owned the information architecture decisions, journey design for both flows, and mid-fidelity prototype delivery.

I collaborated with: product manager (scope), engineering lead (progressive onboarding feasibility), client team (landing strategy alignment).

I did not own: engineering implementation or algorithm weighting — but I designed the UX to surface algorithm output in a way that made its logic legible to users. That boundary matters.

ApproachMethod selection rationale — not a list
Design Sprint (6 days)Stakeholder Interviews × user typeJobs-to-be-DoneSeparate Empathy MapsMid-fi PrototypeModerated Usability Testing

Why a Design Sprint? The client needed stakeholder alignment on priority features before any prototyping began. A sprint compressed 4 weeks of debate into 6 days of structured decisions. The method was itself a strategic intervention — not just a process choice.

Why separate interview sessions per user type? Startups and investors have fundamentally different mental models. Combined sessions produce averaging — not insight. Investors don't want to "browse startups." They want the highest-probability match in the fewest interactions. That reframe changed the entire navigation logic.

[ Startup journey map with friction zones ]
[ Investor journey map with friction zones ]
Key DecisionsJudgment, trade-offs, rationale — this is where seniority shows
01 — Progressive disclosure tied to live match feedback

Staged onboarding so users received a partial match score after each section — creating a visible reason to continue before the full picture emerged.

Higher profile completion rates projected; users had a feedback loop instead of a compliance exercise
Higher engineering complexity; match algorithm needed to surface at multiple flow points, not just post-completion
02 — Bifurcated landing experience per user type

Removed the generic single-hero page. Investors and startups arrive at distinct first screens with distinct value propositions and distinct CTAs.

Higher conversion intent from self-selecting users; eliminated ambiguity about which side of the marketplace you belong to
Reduced single-URL traffic short term; required separate content and SEO strategy per user type
03 — Removed "Browse All" from primary navigation

Listings default to compatibility-ranked view. Open browsing demoted to secondary path — the algorithm's judgment becomes the product's default, not a filter layer.

Increased relevant engagement; positions the platform's intelligence as the core product experience
Some investors expected open browsing; required an additional secondary path to avoid alienating power users
[ Before/After: landing page architecture + navigation decision diagram ]
SolutionCoherence between strategy and output

Five integrated changes that work together as a system:

  • Bifurcated landing with distinct value proposition and CTA per user type
  • Progressive onboarding with live match score preview after each section
  • Investor thesis upload with structured specification criteria (sectors, TRL, geography, ticket size)
  • Compatibility-ranked listings as default — algorithm output surfaced as primary navigation logic
  • UX writing overhaul across tooltips, empty states, error messages — reframed every touchpoint from "form to fill" to "signal to improve"

[ Startup: profile with match score indicator ]
[ Investor: opportunity listing ranked by compatibility ]

RoleLead Researcher & UX Designer
OriginCesar School Master's in Design
RecognitionArmazém da Criatividade Grant
OutputPlatform + Published Paper
se toca
mana!
Context

LBT women in Brazil are systematically absent from preventive healthcare — not because services don't exist, but because the services that exist were never designed with their realities in mind.

This is an epistemic problem before it's a design problem. Health systems encode assumptions about who the default patient is — and LBT women are invisible in those assumptions. The brief wasn't to make a healthcare app. It was to ask: what does a trustworthy interface look like for a community that institutions have repeatedly failed?

AudienceWho participated — and why that was a design decision

Research participants: 6 healthcare professionals and 10 women across public and private health systems in Brazil.

Workshop participants: 12 people from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, gender identities, and sexual orientations — purposefully not a homogeneous sample. Homogeneous samples produce products that work for one group at the expense of everyone else.

Approach
16 Qualitative InterviewsCompetitor BenchmarkingValue Proposition Canvas635 BrainstormingLo-fi WireframingUsability Testing (7+3)Active Listening Workshop

Why low-fi before digital? Paper prototypes make alternatives feel genuinely possible. Digital prototypes anchor people to what already exists. Keeping the prototype on paper during community sessions meant participants felt free to reject ideas entirely.

Why an Active Listening workshop? We needed to surface what wasn't being said in structured interviews. Listening sessions without a predetermined agenda create space for emergent themes — including the rebranding conversation we hadn't anticipated.

[ Workshop documentation · Active Listening Session · 12 participants ]
Key Decision
Rebranding: Se Toca, Mana! → Diversicuida

During the Active Listening Workshop, participants with non-CIS identities shared that the name felt exclusionary. The team rebranded collectively — not to accommodate feedback, but to honour it. The new name, Diversicuida, centers inclusivity structurally, not rhetorically.

This is the most important moment in this case study — not because of the name change, but because of what it demonstrates: co-design as genuine commitment, not as method.

Community trust built through the act of listening and responding — not just through the product itself
Mid-project rebrand: new visual identity, re-testing, updated documentation, additional community alignment sessions

Product Design · ESG
GUIAR
ESG investment management platform. Before designing an impact dashboard, you have to define what impact means.
RoleUX Research Lead · Product Designer
ContextSocial Impact · ESG · Brazil
Output2 Interactive Figma Prototypes
UsersEntrepreneurs + Organizations
Problem

The deeper problem wasn't interface design — it was that users lacked a shared language for measuring socio-environmental impact. Without standardized metrics, any dashboard would only reflect noise.

The design challenge: how do you create a system that imposes just enough structure to enable comparison, without alienating users who resist being measured?

Key Decisions
01 — Two separate prototypes, not one

Entrepreneur and organization flows had fundamentally different mental models: project execution vs. portfolio governance. A unified product would have compromised both.

Validated by testing: users could not easily switch contexts without disorientation
Doubled design effort; required full research cycle for each user type independently
02 — OKR-style impact tracking

Structured measurement around OKRs — a framework users could connect to existing reporting processes — rather than a custom metrics system that required institutional buy-in to adopt.

03 — ESG template gallery

Organized by ESG dimension (Environmental, Social, Governance). This was a content design decision as much as UX — we had to define the taxonomy of impact before we could design the screen.

Framing

The question isn't whether this product works — it's whether this model creates sustainable value, and for whom.

Across 5 programs, my role was never just mentorship. It was ecosystem design: creating the conditions, criteria, and facilitation structures that determine which ideas survive and why.

Programs
Sonae — Corporate Innovation Scouting

Goal: Identify high-potential startups for strategic partnership.
Process: Designed evaluation rubrics across three axes. Ran structured pitch reviews and shortlisting workshops with internal stakeholders.
Impact: Structured criteria reduced evaluation subjectivity; created a reproducible scouting methodology.

SEBRAE — Early-Stage Founder Mentoring

Goal: Help early-stage founders validate business models before pursuing investment.
Process: BMC workshops, customer discovery validation, go-to-market coaching.
Impact: Founders left with testable hypotheses, not just validated assumptions.

CESAR — Intrapreneurship Programs

Goal: Enable internal teams to validate new service concepts.
Process: Designed the facilitation format, ran design thinking sprints, bridged ideation and institutional go/no-go decisions.
Impact: Teams produced prototypes with clear validation criteria.

3-Lens Evaluation Framework

Most frameworks ask two questions: is there a market? is the product viable? I use three:

Desirability
Real evidence of customer pain — or a solution looking for a problem? What was observed vs. assumed?
Viability
Does the model create sustainable value — and for whom does it extract it? Who captures value, and who doesn't?
Systemic Fit ←
Does this strengthen or weaken the ecosystem it enters? Who is excluded by design? Most frameworks skip this entirely.
